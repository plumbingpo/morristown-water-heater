How Bean Station Homeowners Can Extend Water Heater Life

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is simple maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, test the relief valve annually to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which limits scalding risk and slows mineral buildup. Tankless owners should plan on descaling every year if their water is hard.

The age of your heater plays a big role. With regular service, a standard tank usually runs 8 to 12 years, a heat pump water heater 10 to 15, and a tankless unit up to 20. Once a heater passes that range and starts showing warning signs, like rust-colored water, rumbling, or a damp spot under the tank, it's time to plan a replacement. A planned replacement costs less and is far less stressful than scrambling for a new heater after a leak.

Left alone, a small problem tends to grow. Take a spent anode rod: swapping it out is a short, inexpensive repair, but once it's gone, the water begins corroding the tank itself, and suddenly a minor fix becomes a full replacement. That's why we ask homeowners to keep our number in their phone and call at the first sign of trouble. Catching it early saves money and spares you a surprise cleanup.

Typical Water Heater Issues in Bean Station

Most water heater failures we see in Bean Station start with what's in the water. Depending on whether a home is on municipal water or a private well, minerals can be a real factor: calcium and magnesium settle to the bottom of a tank as sediment, insulating the water from the burner, and that's what causes the popping and rumbling you may hear when the heater fires. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, and that's why regular descaling is worth keeping up with in hard-water areas.

Age matters just as much. Plenty of homes around here still have the original tank, and once a tank is past about ten years, the anode rod is usually spent and corrosion begins attacking the steel from the inside. The area has a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, so our technicians handle everything from worn thermocouples and sooty burners to burned-out elements and failed thermostats.

Setup matters too. A home on a private well can see more sediment and sometimes a sulfur smell in the hot water, which a different anode rod often cures. A tankless heater in hard water should get annual descaling. And an older installation getting a new heater often needs updates it never had: an expansion tank, a properly routed relief valve line, a drain pan, or seismic straps where required. Every one of these problems is fixable, and most cost far less when caught early.
